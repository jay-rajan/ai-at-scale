  • At 10k the same 0.7 cut-off merged 99 pairs with 0 wrong. At 100k it is 81% right.
  • The pile changed, not the model: at 10k, 17% of unsure pairs were real duplicates; at 100k, 2%. Rare yeses are harder to get right.
  • Rejecting is where Jev shines: 78,162 rejected, 14 real duplicates lost.
  • Its own label ("same") was worse: 4,236 merges, 2,504 wrong. Use the probability.

What the run shows

  • Code did 99.99% of the work for $0. 5 billion possible pairs down to 84,556 unsure in 19 seconds on a laptop.
  • The model cleared most of the rest cheaply. 84,556 → 5,161 for $1.72 in about 7 minutes.
  • It trades errors, it doesn't remove them. Recall up 1.3 points, precision down 1.2. Wrong groups rose from 334 to 483.
  • A free alarm catches part of it. Groups with two records from one system doubled (99 → 200): a check to run before trusting any merge.
  • 5,161 pairs still need a person. At 5M, the same pipeline leaves 64 million unsure pairs for code to hand over.

Method and limits

  • Data: North Carolina Voters 5M, University of Leipzig, CC BY 4.0. Real voter names with generated duplicates across 5 systems. Sample of whole people, seed 7.
  • Only 4 fields. Some pairs cannot be told apart by anyone: two different people with identical records exist in the data.
  • Score cut-offs were set on the 10k sample and reused unchanged. Jev cut-offs (0.3 / 0.7) were set on 10k labelled pairs.
  • One Jev run. Model typesafe/jev-1.13-20260917 via OpenRouter's alpha Decisions API; cost is what OpenRouter reported per call.
  • Precision and recall are counted on record pairs after grouping, so they differ slightly from the pairwise numbers in earlier steps.
